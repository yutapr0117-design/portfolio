```text
Dear Nick,

Thank you for clarifying.

I understand the importance of respecting the time and attention of people who voluntarily review license discussions. I also understand the value of humans putting real effort into both creating and carefully reading what is submitted.

However, I reject the characterization of ACD as simply an AI-generated license. That does not accurately describe how the current license was created.

AI produced the initial draft, but that draft was simple and merely concretized the design I had specified. I am the designer of ACD.

From that point onward, I determined what I wanted the license to achieve, identified the issues that needed to be addressed, decided the direction of changes, evaluated what should be kept or changed, and controlled the development of the license through prompts and standing instructions.

AI generated much of the wording and performed much of the drafting and analysis, but it did so as a tool implementing my design and direction. I deliberately used AI extensively because it is a powerful tool for turning my design into a concrete and continuously refined text.

The autonomous operation of the repository does not change that relationship. I designed the process so that, once I had defined the direction, constraints, and operating rules, the AI could continue working within them without requiring me to issue a new prompt for every individual step. That made the tool more usable and efficient; it did not transfer design authority from me to the AI.

This role separation is not something I am introducing now in response to this discussion. It has already been part of the documented design of my repository and AI2AI workflow: the human role is system design, architecture, prompt design, orchestration, verification, and final judgment, while AI performs generation and implementation within those human-defined constraints. My existing documentation also explicitly describes the approach as translating human design intent into prompts and treating AI-generated implementation as subordinate to human-defined architecture and structure.

So I did not ask an AI to independently design and create a license and then submit its output for review. I designed the license, used AI extensively to turn that design into its present form, and decided what the license is and what it should become.

I understand ACD and take responsibility for it.

This is also why I think the substance of the resulting work matters. The relevant distinction, in my view, is not simply whether AI generated a large amount of text. Of course it did — I deliberately used the tool extensively. The important question is who designed the work, who directed its development, who understands it, who decides its substance and direction, and who takes responsibility for the resulting contribution.

With that distinction in mind, I would appreciate clarification on one point:

Does OSI consider it inappropriate for a human to design a license and then use AI extensively as a tool to turn that human design into a concrete license text, even where that human controls the direction of the work, determines its substance, understands the resulting license, and takes responsibility for it?

That seems materially different to me from asking an AI to independently design and create a license and then simply submitting its output for others to review.

Best regards,

Yuta Yokoi
```
