Hi Yuta,

I am writing in my capacity as a moderator of the OSI's license mailing lists.

We have noticed that your recent messages on license-review appear highly
structured and may have been substantially generated by an AI system. We also
reviewed the public materials associated with your work and your recent
submission of the Autonomous Commons Dedication 1.0 (ACD-1.0) to
license-discuss. This raised questions about whether your participation may be
part of an experiment or proof of concept involving an AI system autonomously
engaging in license discussions and drafting a license for consideration.

To help us understand the situation, could you please clarify:
- whether your mailing-list messages are drafted or substantially generated by
  an AI system;
- whether an autonomous system is posting or responding directly, or whether you
  personally review and send every message;
- whether ACD-1.0 was drafted wholly or substantially by AI;
- whether testing autonomous participation in the OSI license-review process is
  one of the objectives of this work; and
- whether you personally reviewed, understand, and take responsibility for the
  arguments and license text submitted under your name.

Using AI to help understand legal language, improve writing in a foreign
language, or organize one's thoughts is not necessarily a concern. However,
there is an important distinction between AI assisting an accountable human
participant and an AI system independently generating an extended legal
discussion or using the mailing lists as an experimental environment without
disclosure.

The license-review and license-discuss lists depend on participants engaging in
good faith, accurately representing the nature of their contributions, and
taking personal responsibility for the positions and texts they submit.
Undisclosed autonomous participation could consume substantial reviewer time and
affect the integrity of the review process.

We would therefore appreciate a clear explanation of how AI is being used in your
participation and what role you personally play in reviewing and approving the
content. Please reply directly to this message before making further submissions
related to ACD-1.0.

Kind regards,
Nick

Hi Nick,

Yes. Since you asked me to explain this in as much detail as I can, I will try to
describe the whole structure clearly: who I am, what the repository is doing, why
I operate it this way, how ACD emerged, and how all of that relates to my
participation on the OSI mailing lists.

First, the most basic point: I am a real human being, Yuta Yokoi.

The GitHub account yutapr0117-design is mine, the portfolio repository is mine,
and the messages I send to OSI under my name are sent by me. I am the human owner
and governance/decision-making party for the repository, and I am the person who
takes responsibility for external statements and for decisions concerning ACD.

I am attaching my Japanese resume and curriculum vitae as additional identity
evidence. These are ordinary documents I have used for employment purposes. They
were prepared when I was joining my current employer, so they predate my current
employment and therefore do not contain information about my present role. I am
attaching them simply to remove any ambiguity about whether there is a real human
person behind this activity.

The GitHub repository and my OSI mailing-list participation are related, but they
are not the same system and they do not have the same autonomy boundary.

WHAT THE PORTFOLIO REPOSITORY IS

The repository is:

https://github.com/yutapr0117-design/portfolio

It is my public portfolio. Its original purpose is literally self-branding:
showing what I build, how I work, and how I use AI.

From the first commit, implementation has been AI-driven. I have never
independently written implementation code for the repository myself. There have
been a small number of cases where I edited AI configuration files exactly as the
AI instructed me to do, but I did not originate or hand-write the implementation
itself. If those AI-directed edits are counted as part of the AI workflow, then
the implementation history is effectively AI-generated from the beginning.

The repository later evolved into what I call a self-driving repository. Roughly
speaking, it was AI-only from commit zero, and it became self-driving in the
stronger sense around the time the AI-to-AI handoff structure became mature,
approximately around the first 80 commits.

Today, the self-driving operation of this personal repository is mainly performed
by Claude Code using Anthropic's Opus model. Because this is my personal
environment on the lowest-cost plan, it normally runs as a single session rather
than multiple parallel sessions.

The important point is that I do not give an instruction for every commit.

The AI can inspect the repository, identify possible improvements, decide what to
work on, implement changes, test them, inspect CI, fix failures, update
documentation, update handoff material and evidence, select the next area to
inspect, and sometimes decide that no change is necessary.

It can create branches, push, create pull requests, merge, and continue the
improvement cycle without asking me for approval for each individual repository
operation.

My top-level constraints are intentionally simple:

1. Do not break existing behavior.
2. Keep CI green.

Within those two constraints, I do not impose an additional human-approval
requirement for each repository action.

That is the sense in which I broadly delegate the repository to AI. It does not
mean that there are no quality constraints; the two constraints above are the
governing constraints, and the repository itself contains many checks and
operating rules that have accumulated over time.

This is also why the GitHub activity can be very high even when I personally do
almost nothing manually. The activity is not produced in order to make the
repository look busy. It is a consequence of continuous AI-driven inspection,
implementation, validation, documentation, auditing, correction and improvement.

I do not know every current implementation detail or every commit in real time.
That is intentional. In this operating model, the source of truth is not my
memory. It is the repository itself: commits, pull requests, CI, documentation,
handoff material and evidence.

The repository contains structures such as AI2AI, CLAUDE-related instructions,
handoff material, governance documents, documentation and evidence. AI2AI in
particular expresses the repository's basic philosophy and operating direction so
that a later AI session does not have to understand everything again from zero.

The repository also has a large and continuously evolving set of CI checks,
consistency checks, behavioral checks, mutation-style tests, review mechanisms,
evidence records and other quality gates. I am deliberately not giving you a fixed
current number here because the repository changes continuously and I do not
manually track that count. The repository itself is the authoritative source for
the current number and composition.

The same applies to provenance. External review material can be tied to the
specific artifact that was reviewed; evidence and audit records are retained; and
the repository distinguishes external review from its own internal AI analysis.
More recently, it has also started pinning reviewed artifacts and preserving
mutable external source material where necessary so that later analysis can still
identify what was actually reviewed or quoted at the time.

FAILURES AND CORRECTIONS

I do not assume that AI is infallible. Humans are not infallible either.

When the system finds an error, a bad assumption, stale information, a bad
measurement, an invalid check, or a process weakness, the normal pattern is to
correct it and add what was learned back into the repository so that the same
class of failure is less likely to recur.

I normally do not erase the history by pretending the failure never happened.
Failures are part of the public history. The normal pattern is forward correction
rather than hiding the mistake.

This is important to me because a system that only preserves successful outputs
but hides its failed reasoning would be much less useful as a reproducible
engineering system.

HOW ACD EMERGED

ACD began as the custom LICENSE of this portfolio repository.

The direct origin was actually simple. Another AI told me about custom licenses
and the possibility of creating one. I then told the AI that I wanted my own
license to aim for external approval.

I did not begin by writing a detailed legal specification myself. I did not
provide a large list of initial legal requirements. I gave the goal, and the AI
generated and developed the license inside the same delegated operating model
used by the repository.

When it later became appropriate to work on a next version, I essentially told
the AI that creating a next version was fine.

The central intent that I care about is that ACD should be genuinely permissive
for AI use as well as ordinary software use. In other words, I believe an AI-era
license that does not add AI-specific downstream restrictions is useful and worth
trying to standardize.

The ACD text, comparisons with existing licenses, consistency analysis, revision
proposals, reviewer-response material, evidence, audit records and related
documentation have therefore been generated and improved largely inside this
AI-delegated workflow.

That does not mean I treat every sentence produced by AI as legally correct
simply because AI produced it.

The repository checks contradictions, tests interpretations, incorporates external
feedback, distinguishes AI review from human legal review or OSI review, and
revises material when problems are found.

I have not yet commissioned a formal legal review. If targeted professional legal
review becomes necessary in order to obtain OSI approval, I am willing to obtain
it at that point.

My goal is not to prove that an AI can autonomously participate in OSI.

My goal is to make ACD a real license that can be reviewed, corrected where
necessary, approved if it satisfies the requirements, recognized in systems such
as SPDX, and eventually adopted and used by third parties.

THE OSI MAILING LISTS ARE DIFFERENT

This is the most important boundary.

The GitHub repository may operate with a high degree of autonomy, but my
participation on the OSI mailing lists does not work that way.

AI does not independently post to the OSI mailing lists as me.

Every message that is sent under my name is ultimately sent by me.

I read the message before sending it. I understand what I am sending. I accept
responsibility for the content.

Sometimes I write the English myself.

Sometimes I write the underlying idea or a draft and use AI or a translation tool
to improve, organize or translate it.

Sometimes an AI produces most of the wording and I review it.

Sometimes I edit it substantially, sometimes only slightly, and sometimes not at
all if it already expresses exactly what I mean.

The level of AI involvement therefore varies from message to message.

I use AI in writing because, although I can write the material myself, it is often
simply faster and less tedious to have AI draft, organize or translate it.
Sometimes I do not use AI at all.

So I do not consider 'AI-generated wording' and 'autonomous AI participation' to
be the same thing.

The distinction I care about is:

- who decides what is being said;
- who understands it;
- who sends it; and
- who accepts responsibility for it.

For messages I send to OSI under my name, that accountable participant is me.

HOW THE GITHUB ACTIVITY AND OSI INTERACTION CONNECT

There is information flow between the repository and my OSI participation, but
there is not an autonomous closed loop in which the repository independently
participates in OSI.

Information from OSI discussions, reviews and archives may be shown to the AI, or
the AI may retrieve publicly available material and use it in repository analysis
or improvements.

Likewise, analysis accumulated in the repository may help me prepare a
mailing-list message or response.

When relevant, I have also shown the repository AI original mailing-list material
and email exchanges. For example, when I showed it the exchange explaining that at
least two substantive review messages had been rejected in moderation, it
incorporated that fact into its understanding of the review process.

I may give the AI important context, but I intentionally do not always tell it
what conclusion it must reach. If I give it the answer in advance, it simply
follows my answer and the self-driving process loses much of its value.

The practical relationship is approximately:

external discussion / review / relevant email material
-> AI and repository analysis
-> repository improvement and/or draft material
-> I review what I intend to say externally
-> I personally send the external message
-> later external feedback may return to the repository and become new input.

I do not necessarily know every internal step the AI takes in that process,
because the repository operation itself is broadly delegated.

But the boundary at external communication is clear: a message sent under my name
is my message.

Some AI environments I use can access certain external services directly and
others cannot. That technical capability is not what determines responsibility in
my workflow. Regardless of capability, I do not treat an external statement under
my name as autonomous AI speech.

MY REVIEWS OF OTHER LICENSES

My participation on the OSI lists is not limited to ACD.

I review other licenses because I find license design interesting.

Normally I see licenses appear on the mailing list and participate when one
catches my interest. OpenMDW, ModelGo and BOS are examples.

Those are genuine reviews. They are not fake activity designed to make ACD appear
more credible.

Sometimes I first identify a review point and AI helps me organize or verify it.
Sometimes AI identifies something and I investigate it. The direction is
bidirectional.

Knowledge from those reviews can later be useful to ACD, just as analysis
developed around ACD can make it easier to understand another license. The
repository also appears to incorporate useful information from public archives
into its own knowledge base as part of its normal self-driving operation.

That is normal learning and reuse of knowledge. Other submitters are not
experimental subjects for ACD.

WHAT IS AND IS NOT AN EXPERIMENT

There is one broad sense in which this activity is experimental: I am genuinely
interested in seeing how far one human and AI can go together.

But the work itself is always real.

ACD is not a fake submission.

My reviews of other licenses are not fake reviews.

My questions are not fake questions.

OSI participants are not test subjects.

I did not participate in the OSI mailing lists in order to run a proof of concept
for autonomous AI participation.

The workflow may have become an interesting experiment in human-AI collaboration,
but I am seriously trying to obtain approval for a license that I believe is
useful.

WHY I AM DOING THIS

The reasons are simple.

I think a genuinely permissive license for AI-era use is necessary.

I do not want ACD to remain merely a private custom LICENSE inside my repository.
I want it to be understood externally, seriously reviewed, improved where
reviewers identify real problems, approved if it satisfies the requirements, made
machine-readable where possible, and ultimately adopted and used by other people.

I use a self-driving repository because I do not want to type a separate prompt
for every small task. If the AI can continuously inspect, improve, validate,
document and learn from the repository, then I can remain focused on direction,
external decisions, exceptions, and responsibility for statements made under my
name.

The repository is public because it is a portfolio and self-branding project. I
also do not want the system to depend only on undocumented knowledge in my head.
If it became completely person-dependent and unreproducible, much of the point
would be lost.

RESPONSIBILITY

I want to make one point especially clear.

The fact that I do not personally know every commit does not mean that there is no
accountable human behind my external participation.

Repository operation, repository ownership, authorship or generation of individual
artifacts, mailing-list participation, and stewardship/decision-making for a
license are different roles.

I delegate a large amount of technical work.

I do not delegate away responsibility for statements I personally send.

If I send an email to OSI, I accept responsibility for that email.

If I formally submit ACD, the decision to submit it is mine.

If OSI identifies a problem and I need to decide whether to accept a change,
revise the license, continue, or withdraw, the final decision is mine.

The same is true of the broader direction: the AI can propose, analyze, draft and
implement, but I remain the human owner and the person responsible for the
external decision.

WHAT I WOULD LIKE YOU TO EVALUATE

I do not think a message should be accepted merely because a human typed every
character manually.

Likewise, I do not think a substantive contribution should be rejected merely
because AI helped produce the wording.

What I would prefer is that the actual contribution be judged on its substance:
relevance, correctness, usefulness, whether the sender understands what is being
said, whether the contribution is made in good faith, and whether there is an
accountable participant behind it.

That is the role I am trying to fulfill.

So, in short:

- I am a real human person.
- The GitHub repository is highly autonomous and AI-operated.
- Its implementation has been AI-driven from the beginning, and it became
  self-driving in the stronger sense after the AI2AI structure matured.
- The AI can perform repository work, including push/PR/merge, without
  per-operation human approval as long as it preserves existing behavior and keeps
  CI green.
- I do not personally track every commit or every implementation detail.
- The repository itself preserves the authoritative history, checks,
  documentation, handoff and evidence.
- My OSI mailing-list participation is different: I review and personally send
  messages under my name and accept responsibility for them.
- AI may draft, translate, organize or improve wording, and the amount of AI
  involvement varies from message to message.
- ACD is a genuine attempt to create and standardize a useful license, not a
  simulation of a license submission.
- My reviews of other licenses are genuine reviews because I find the subject
  interesting, not activity manufactured to support ACD.
- I am interested in how far one human and AI can go together, but I am not using
  OSI or its participants as experimental subjects.

If there is any specific part of this workflow you would like me to explain or
