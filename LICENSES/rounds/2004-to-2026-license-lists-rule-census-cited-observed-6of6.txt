As with your previous versions, this license is very poorly drafted, 
making it difficult to understand. As a major flaw that makes it 
unacceptable for approval, I do not see where the license grants the 
right to modify the software. I may have missed it because the license 
is so disorganized, but I see the right to convey verbatim copies in 
section 16, and I see the right to convey modified copies in 2, but not 
the right to modify the software, or to exercise any rights of a 
copyright owner other than convey.

There are very confusing overlapping terms and repetition of phrases 
that make it almost impossible to parse this license. For example, you 
have defined "Source Code" and "Corresponding Source" in ways that 
overlap: "'Source Code' ... includes all files, interface definitions, 
scripts, build instructions ...", and "'Corresponding Source' includ[es] 
build scripts, control files, interface definitions, and configuration 
files that are specifically required for those purposes. ¶ Corresponding 
Source includes scripts and configuration files specifically required to 
build, install, and run the Work itself." If Corresponding Source is a 
subset of Source Code, but Source Code includes all files, interface 
definitions, etc., then why are similar terms also used in the 
definition of Corresponding Source? Are they meant to be different? And 
why does the definition of Corresponding Source talk about "build 
scripts, control files, interface definitions, and configuration files" 
in one spot and "scripts and configuration files specifically required 
to build, install, and run the Work" in another? Is there a difference? 
If they are different, why aren't these all in the same sentence?

There are internal inconsistencies. For example, you say "If the Work is 
Conveyed in Source Code form, the Source code itself constitutes the 
Corresponding Source." But does it? If it's missing /all/ files (of 
what, I'm not sure), interface definitions, scripts and build 
instructions, how can it nevertheless be Corresponding Source? Or are 
you saying that it should be deemed adequate as Corresponding Source 
even if it's missing all that stuff?

Your changes to the anti-circumvention provision of the AGPL make your 
version of the provision largely ineffective. As I read the AGPL (and 
happy to be corrected), the paragraph prevents someone /other than/ the 
licensor from adding anti-circumvention measures, e.g., a commercial 
company conveys a software program to its customers and wraps it in DRM. 
Your revision to this concept limits the restriction to only the 
licensor, someone who is unlikely to impose DRM.

The above is a very limited set of problems with the license and by no 
means comprehensive. It's so disorganized with unnecessary complexities 
that, in my opinion, it's not ready for consideration by OSI.

As mentioned to you in the past, a license of this complexity needs to 
be drafted by a professional.

I have the following comments on the license:

OSI will not approve licenses where the title refers to a particular 
person, entity or software or that have them "hard coded" into the 
license (see "Standard for New Licenses" para. 1, 
https://opensource.org/licenses/review-process). For this reason, the 
license name and the text of the license cannot include "CompanionNation."

The license does not grant all the rights under copyright, at least 
under US law. US law also includes the right to publicly perform and 
publicly display. This matters because, as an example, use in SaaS might 
be considered a public performance or display of the software. That 
said, it is also better practice not to define the the grant by 
enumerating specific rights, since those may be country-specific, and 
instead state more generally that all necessary rights have been granted 
subject to the conditions of the license.

The definition of "Software" is ambiguous. It is defined as software 
that "includ[es] any modifications or derivative works." This "or" 
statement suggests that modifications are different from derivative 
works, but there is no description or definition of a "modification." 
This matters because in the grant clause in some places you have granted 
the right to make derivative works but not modifications. For this 
reason too you have not necessarily granted all rights under copyright.

You say that the license is a "simple permissive license," but the 
definition of "Software" can lead to a conclusion that it is a copyleft 
license.  If I modify the "Software," and "Software" includes "any 
modifications or derivative works," are my modifications now subject to 
this license?

Also, the possibility that "modifications" that are not within the scope 
of copyright may nevertheless become subject to the license may violate 
OSD 9.

You have overstated what you perceive to be one flaw with the Apache 
license, which is that the Apache license "imposes a NOTICE file 
requirement." It is true that IF a notice file exists there are 
obligations for others to provide it, but there is no duty for the 
original licensor to include a NOTICE file ("*/If /*the Work includes a 
'NOTICE' text file as part of its distribution ..."). You can avoid the 
complexity of the NOTICE file requirement by not including one.

You also say that a flaw of the MIT license is that it "requires 
retention of the copyright notice and permission notice only," 
suggesting that a copy of the license is not required. That is not 
accurate; the "permission notice" that you mention must be provided 
*/IS/* the license.  Thus, the gap that you claim exists between your 
proposed license and the MIT and Apache licenses is not as broad as you 
claim.

Please also note, per the license review guidelines, "a license cannot 
be changed while it is being considered. If the license submitter would 
like to change the language of the license, the current version of the 
license should be withdrawn from review and an updated version 
submitted. We recommend that, if changes are going to be made, that the 
license submitter wait and collect all the desired changes in a single 
new submission rather than withdrawing and resubmitting the same license 
several times."

Just to be clear, the OSI already is supposed to consider "software
freedom", as I noted earlier, as stated at
https://opensource.org/licenses/review-process

The purpose of OSI license review is, in part, to "Ensure approved
licenses conform to the Open Source Definition *and* provide software
freedom".
(emphasis added - the important thing is that "conformance to the OSD"
and "providing software freedom" are not equivalent)

and

"[The license-review participants'] consensus that a license does not
ensure software freedom may, in some cases, be the justification for
rejecting a license even where they cannot identify a specific aspect
of the OSD or the approval guidelines below that is not met."

That's not an articulated "test" but since adoption of that language
in . . . 2019 or so? . . . the OSI has at least in some cases made
reference to the concept of "software freedom" in explaining its
dispositions of particular submitted licenses. And I explained the
historical reason for this: Artful license drafters were arguing that
the OSI was compelled to approve any license with some sort of novel
controversial provision if a clear conflict with a specific OSD
provision could not be identified. In some respects this is a
recognition of problems with the OSD itself.
