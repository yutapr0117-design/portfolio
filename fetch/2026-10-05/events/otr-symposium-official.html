About the Symposium

The Open Technology Research (OTR) Symposium, themed “Shaping the Open Transition”, moves from diagnosis to action: from mapping the landscape to charting the path forward. With keynotes anchored in what is already working, and sessions designed to translate research directly into policy recommendations and practice, the event is both a stocktaking exercise and a forward-looking call to build a more united, evidence-based global research agenda – one that strengthens open technology’s role as a shared foundation for resilience and sovereignty, reinforces public interest governance, and mobilises research and evidence to drive collective action.

For more on the OFA Symposium, the predecessor to the OTR Symposium, visit the archived website for the event.

What is Open Technology?

‘Open technology’ does not have a single agreed-upon definition, and the distinction is not merely semantic. It has real consequences for what gets researched, who gets included, and whose priorities shape the field. Many practitioners of ‘open technology’ use and apply the term differently – open source software, open standards, open hardware, open data, open science infrastructure, etc. – are all generally agreed upon as domains of open technology, but openness can mean different things in each case.

Open Technology Research works with a broad and deliberately pluralistic definition of open technology, encompassing the humans, knowledge assets, software, hardware, standards, data, and the governance and licensing frameworks that structure access to each. What unites the field is not a single definition but a shared set of questions about who controls the technologies societies depend on, who benefits from and contributes to their development, and what governance arrangements make those relationships more equitable and sustainable.

Fireside Chat – Navigating the Open Transition (Sasha Costanza-Chock)

Open technologies like source software, open data, and open knowledge increasingly underpin much of the technology that shapes public life, but who controls that technology, and on what terms, remains contested. This is particularly relevant as society navigates a transition wherein open technology is increasingly leveraged at scale, particularly in support of digital sovereignty, but without adequate understanding of its role as a shared foundation for public interest governance. In this Fireside Chat, Sasha Costanza-Chock discusses the contours of the transition — where it is happening, where it is lagging behind, what open technology requires, and who open technology should serve. The conversation will explore topics such as: why the new infrastructure for democratic deliberation needs to be open; whether ethical use clauses in licences need to be rethought; and how the open community should respond when companies build closed systems on top of openly shared knowledge. It will also address a current test case: whether AI developed in the public interest must be open source, and how that position holds up against concerns about misuse.

Governance Before Technology: Lessons from Integrate Pasifika for Open Knowledge Infrastructure and Digital Sovereignty in the Pacific

This talk presents a paper arguing that open technology initiatives fail more often through inadequate governance than technical shortcomings, using Integrate Pasifika, an SPC-led initiative connecting Communities of Practice and Indigenous Knowledge Management Systems across Pacific Island Countries and Territories. Drawing on the 2024 co-design workshop report, 2025 technical design notes, and Langton et al.’s Framework for the Governance of Indigenous Data, it identifies five themes: interoperability across fragmented national systems, governance before platform functionality, country ownership and sovereignty, capacity constraints as infrastructure gaps, and Communities of Practice as governance mechanisms. Linking Ostrom, Wenger, and Langton et al. to debates on open technology governance, it derives principles of knowledge asset recognition, co-governance, capability development, sovereignty-preserving openness, and inclusive ecosystem design. It concludes that success depends on stewardship, legitimacy, participation, sustained capability investment, and balancing openness with local authority.

How Open Should We Go? A Decision Framework for Open Sourcing, InnerSourcing, or Closing Public Sector Code

Public sector organisations increasingly accept the case for open source, yet few open source everything, and research offers little guidance on how such disclosure decisions are or should be made. Choosing between open source, InnerSource and closed code depends less on technical factors than on return on investment, organisational readiness, regulatory obligations, and whether collaboration overhead is justified for transitional code or code unsuited to public consumption. Drawing on ongoing joint research with government OSPOs and the InnerSource Commons community, this talk presents an emerging framework that treats these options as a continuum of code stewardship, identifying what moves projects along it and what legitimately keeps them closed. It offers researchers a way to study disclosure decisions as governance arrangements, and decision-makers a practical tool for defensible choices about when openness serves the public interest and when it does not.

 Governance

Reconceptualizing data governance as the interplay between increasing and decreasing openness

This talk presents a paper on the Openness Dynamics Framework (ODF), developed in Data for Democracy – Asia (D4D Asia), a seven-country study in South and Southeast Asia, to study the hard-to-define concept of data governance systematically. The ODF treats data governance as the interplay between technologies, laws, policies and practices that promote openness, such as open standards, free and open source software, right to information, open data, open access, open APIs and consent-based sharing, and those that restrict it, such as secrecy laws, data protection, data localisation, copyright, patents and surveillance. It shows that openness has many dimensions to unpack, and that good data governance needs instruments of both kinds. Applying it, D4D Asia found an asymmetry: states promoted openness mostly through judicially unenforceable policies but restricted it through enforceable laws.

Digital Commons as Civic Infrastructure: Governance Frameworks for Open Source AI and Public Accountability

As AI spreads through public administration in the Global South, dependence on proprietary, opaque algorithms threatens public interest governance, and this talk goes beyond diagnosing algorithmic bias to show how open source software and digital assets can be governed as civic infrastructure for state accountability and sovereignty. Because concentrated compute and data give AI new routes to corporate capture beyond typical community-firm models, it proposes design criteria for a Public-Commons Partnership (PCP) through which governments, ministries, national budgets, and civic tech networks co-steward open infrastructure. Drawing on field observations from African public sector deployments in agriculture, public registries and healthcare, it turns practitioner insights into evidence for decision-makers. Responding to the OTR call to action, it offers a non-technical policy blueprint for procurement and oversight that makes openness a non-negotiable requirement for democratic resilience.

Extending digital sovereignty beyond software: the role of open source hardware

This talk presents a paper arguing that digital sovereignty, usually addressed through software, data and platforms, ultimately depends on hardware design, manufacturing and governance, and so requires extending open principles to open source hardware (OSH). Based on desk research, surveys and expert interviews, it describes five interdependent areas of OSH: open source silicon, data centre and networking hardware, electronics, mechanical hardware and scientific hardware. It finds that dependence on proprietary design tools, manufacturing and global supply chains limits control even where software is open, while open standards, shared designs and distributed manufacturing let each area contribute to sovereignty, from compute architectures to tools for local innovation and scientific reproducibility. It concludes that sovereignty is a full-stack concern and that OSH is a necessary extension of open source software, supporting more resilient, transparent, and collaboratively governed digital public infrastructure.

14:00 – 14:30

Mapping the OSH Manufacturing Landscape: Facilitative Models for Open Source Hardware in Europe and Beyond

Open source hardware (OSH) is key to open technology but underexplored, and unlike open source software, little is known about how it is disseminated and manufactured beyond research institutions- a research gap and policy priority as European policy stresses strategic autonomy and local production. This talk presents a paper mapping the intermediation models, such as commercial manufacturers, brokerage networks and consortium-based vendors, that link OSH researchers with manufacturers, their dynamics and readiness for distribution, and how tech transfer offices could support the pathway to manufacturing. The qualitative study combines desk research and interviews with organisations including Open Science Shop, which brokers projects to manufacturers, LABmaker, which manufactures locally, and CERN’s White Rabbit project, which tenders mature OSH to vendors. It evaluates the sustainability of their business models against the need for scientific reproducibility and local production capacity in Europe and beyond, and suggests support mechanisms to make OSH a strategic pillar of open technology policy.

Keynote – What Public Software Can Teach Us: Lessons for Open Technology Researchers from Studying Software Heritage (Stefano Zacchiroli)

Software Heritage has assembled the largest existing archive of publicly available software source code and its development history: more than 20 billion unique source code files and 5 billion commits, harvested from over 400 million projects. Its purpose is preservation, but it comes with an important side effect: for the first time, the entire body of public software is available as a single, uniform, openly accessible object of study, rather than scattered across platforms.

In this talk, Stefano Zacchiroli, one of the co-founders of Software Heritage, will show what that makes possible, through recent research results obtained using the archive as a large-scale dataset: tracking where code actually comes from and where it travels, measuring the health and long-term sustainability of software commons, studying the people who write and maintain code and the gaps in who gets to do so, and establishing the provenance of the code on which today’s AI models are trained. In doing so, he will expand on what open technology researchers can learn from his experiences and why rigorous, empirically grounded research on open technology is critical for shaping the open transition.

 Sustainability and Business Models

From Volunteer Heroics to Public Infrastructure: A Talent-Pool Model for Funding Disaster-Response Open Technology

This talk argues that Taiwan’s civic technology response to recent disasters reveals a structural paradox in open technology sustainability: the tools that save the most lives are the least funded. In 2025, a volunteer built an asbestos-waste tracking map overnight after Typhoon Danas, unpaid and disconnected from upstream decisions, and citizen platforms coordinated 500,000 volunteer visits after a landslide dam overflow near Guangfu while information stalled between government layers – cases where the public sector gains value while creators bear the cost, risk, and invisibility. The talk presents three mechanisms under design by Taiwanese communities and government: a Disaster Resilience Civic Tech Talent Pool offering vetted contributors per-diem compensation, insurance, and accountable renewal; an “IT Squad” embedding them in Emergency Operations Centers under a government MOU; and a procurement-funded Shared Component Library of twelve MIT-licensed, security-audited modules for any volunteer team. Drawing on interviews with national disaster agencies, two municipal governments, and civic tech communities, it offers a transferable funding and governance model that makes the exchange between value and contribution accountable for any country whose open technology ecosystem is under-resourced relative to its value in crises.

Building a Method: Challenges and Lessons from Developing an Impact Evaluation Framework for Open Source Software Funding

As open source software (OSS) is increasingly funded and governed as public infrastructure, funders face growing demands for accountability, transparency and evidence of impact, yet evaluation methods are themselves research infrastructure, dependent on the same open tools, data and standards as the fragile systems they assess. OSS impact evaluation remains an emerging research area with no widely accepted methodological standard, and approaches such as ROI models, DevOps Research Assessment, COCOMO and underproduction risk models rely on simplifying assumptions about project structure and code history that do not fully capture OSS ecosystems. The Sovereign Tech Agency has so far relied on qualitative reporting and grantee self-assessment, which provide rich context but are hard to compare, aggregate at portfolio level, or use for evidence-based funding decisions, so its Research & Policy team is developing a quantitative impact evaluation framework. This talk presents how the framework was built, from motivation and design to the practical and structural challenges encountered, and argues that evaluating OSS funding is a governance practice as well as a methodological problem, depending on open research infrastructure, community knowledge, and continuous methodological iteration.

Deploying Open Technology in Development: A Complementarities Approach

Development actors have embraced open technologies for digital public infrastructure (DPI) such as payments, identity, and registries, funding open source “building blocks” to cut costs, reduce vendor lock-in, and increase speed, yet progress has been slow or fragile, and ecosystems remain dependent on outside funding. This talk hypothesises that open technology successes rest on thick complements, such as technical labour markets, corporate sponsors, universities, procurement, service firms and motivated contributors, that let value be captured locally and recycled into maintenance. In lower-income settings, these complements are often thin or missing, so stewardship costs fall back on funders and narrow technology investments rarely become sustainable. Comparing large-scale open technology ecosystems with DPI such as MOSIP and Mojaloop, the research will examine how open technology has been resourced, which complements must be funded alongside it, and which options suit development contexts.

A Multidimensional Assessment Framework for Evaluating the Sustainability of Open Infrastructures

Open infrastructures, built on principles of openness, collaboration, transparency and community governance, support a common ecosystem for creating, disseminating, preserving, discovering and reusing scholarly knowledge, enabling equitable access to research and strengthening innovation, interoperability and digital resilience. This talk presents a study assessing their sustainability through an Open Infrastructure Sustainability Index (OISI) with four dimensions: Community Engagement, Governance Practices, Organisational Capacity and Development Activity. The study draws on data for 137 infrastructures from the Invest in Open Infrastructure (IOI) Infra Finder, combined with GitHub repository analytics and manually collected data for repositories hosted elsewhere, excluding those with insufficient data or no public repository. Descriptive analysis of the resulting scores identifies sustainability patterns, and the OISI offers a systematic way to evaluate and compare open infrastructures across multiple dimensions.

A Blueprint for Open Science? Slovenia’s Legislative Path to Rights Retention and the Secondary Publication Right

Policymakers across Europe have developed legal mechanisms such as rights-retention policies and statutory secondary publication rights to ensure public access to publicly funded research, but reforms often meet resistance when they require amending copyright law. This talk examines Slovenia’s approach, which advances Open Science through research-specific legislation, namely Article 41 of the Scientific Research and Innovation Activity Act and the Decree on Open Science in Scientific Research, rather than copyright reform. Slovenia first introduced a mandatory rights-retention obligation for recipients of public research funding, then a statutory secondary publication right allowing researchers to make their works openly accessible regardless of contractual restrictions. By embedding these obligations and rights in research and funding legislation, Slovenia avoided many obstacles to copyright reform, offering a potentially transferable model for EU Member States and other jurisdictions considering secondary publication rights.

 Research Infrastructure

Reclone: Open Research Infrastructure for Equitable Access to Biotechnology Reagents

This talk examines the Reagent Collaboration Network (Reclone), a global partnership building distributed infrastructure to increase access to molecular biology reagents, as a case study of open biomaterials and of open research infrastructure beyond digital technologies. Reclone’s model combines co-developing open source biotechnologies, which involves IP screening, de novo DNA synthesis and distribution under the OpenMTA alongside documentation, technical standards and community coordination; building decentralised distribution and production; creating open access training resources; and engaging enterprise while keeping open technology and affordable access at its core. Regional Hubs in Latin America, Africa and Southeast Asia distribute to local researchers, coordinate training, adapt protocols and support regulatory compliance, while turning users into contributors remains difficult given the costs of R&D, and collaboration with local manufacturers such as NexEnzymes in Chile is producing new open protocols, kits and methods. The talk presents the technology, policy, and legal factors shaping this work, quantitative and qualitative impact data, and future governance and sustainability challenges that could inform other initiatives.

Inside the InnerSource Program Office: An Activity Theory Analysis of How Organizations Enable Open Collaboration at Scale

InnerSource, the application of open source principles within organisations, is increasingly used to improve collaboration, code reuse, and developer engagement, and as a step towards open source; yet the InnerSource Program Offices (ISPOs) that coordinate it remain unexplored, and their relationship to Open Source Program Offices (OSPOs) has never been empirically examined. This talk presents a qualitative study of ten organisations across sectors and geographies, including the public sector, that uses Activity Theory to examine ISPO activities, the blockers constraining them, and their interaction with OSPOs. Like OSPOs, ISPOs act as boundary-spanning systems that create resources and tooling, support practitioners, engage leadership, and build community, but they must also build community, incentives, and legitimacy within organisational boundaries, and their relationship with OSPOs ranges from overlapping to distinct, shifting where InnerSource is framed as a stepping stone to open source. Interpreting blockers such as cultural resistance and proprietary-oriented compliance rules as Activity Theory contradictions, the study positions the ISPO as a distinct mechanism related to but not reducible to the OSPO, and offers guidance on designing and sustaining both functions together.

 Behavioural Change/Adoption

Open Source as Co-Creation: Lessons on Adoption and Public Product Ownership from Cameroon’s One Health Information System

Open technology projects in government often face their greatest barriers in institutional adoption and user ownership rather than software development, and this talk examines the implementation of the Cameroon One Health Information System (COHIS), an open source platform integrating human, animal, and environmental health data, to identify what supported behavioural change and adoption. Drawing on implementation experience, it considers how technology choice, sequencing, user participation and capacity development shaped uptake, identifying five mutually reinforcing factors: starting with concrete surveillance and coordination use cases; incremental implementation that let users refine requirements; government analysts configuring data flows, dashboards and access rules themselves; capacity development embedded in work with real data; and self-service tools with a modular architecture that let users adapt analyses, work with different partners and progressively govern the system. Open source suited COHIS because the platform manages sensitive government data and needs flexibility across sectors. The case study concludes that adoption was strengthened when openness was combined with co-creation, work-embedded capacity development, operational relevance, and the institutional ability to shape the product beyond the initial implementation and future funding cycles.

 Behavioural Change/Adoption

Political User Experience (PUX): Reimagining User Experience for Digital Participatory Policymaking

Research on open technologies has traditionally focused on reducing barriers to participation through usability, accessibility and user experience (Norman, 2013; Hassenzahl, 2010), while digital democracy research has examined institutional design, civic participation and democratic innovations (Fung, 2006; Smith, 2009; Escobar & Elstub, 2019). Much less attention has been paid to how interaction design shapes political agency, collective sense-making and the democratic quality of participation. Building on the Horizon Europe INSPIRE project and the concept of Political User Experience (PUX) (Schulbaum & Nadesan, 2025), this talk presents a paper arguing that digital participation platforms should be understood as digital infrastructures for participatory policymaking rather than online consultation tools. Instead of treating citizens as users or consumers, PUX examines how platforms mediate power, access and agency, positioning them as urban assemblages of technologies, norms, bodies, and institutions that together shape democratic participation.

 Public Procurement Ecosystem

From Open Source to Open State: Shifting the Pareto Frontier of Public Procurement

Public sector innovation has long been constrained by a tradeoff between accountability and speed, felt most in procurement during crisis and change, when rapid procurement trades accountability for adaptability at the risk of fraud and lost public trust, while smaller vendors offering boundary-pushing solutions rarely fit traditional pathways and are most exposed to compliance and accounting costs. This talk explores new coordination and trust technologies, particularly independent verifiability at machine speed, that shift the Pareto frontier of procurement to be both more accountable and more adaptive. When verification is independent, cheap, and as fast as disruptive technologies such as AI, more open procurement models become possible that let governments use other technologies rather than suppress them over security concerns. The talk walks through case studies on converting policy to code, outcomes-based contracting, and government-to-government (G2G) markets in which administrations procure in-house solutions from each other, tracing this shift across policymaking, technology, and governance alongside existing policies and the policy changes needed to support it.

Track:
 Public Procurement Ecosystem

The Ecosystem Blueprint: Reframing Vendor Survival Psychology in Digital Procurement

Government IT procurement consistently fails through a cognitive category error rather than technical incompetence: it treats digital infrastructure, a “living forest” of shared code, open APIs and fluid collaboration, as static “Industrial Machine” parts sourced through single-winner competitions. Forced into this frame, multi-vendor teams resort to survival mechanisms such as hoarding intellectual property, siloing information, and letting adjacent sprints fail to maximise their own contracts. This talk introduces “coopetition” mechanics that shift multi-vendor environments towards cooperative stewardship, drawing on Mark Schwartz’s case study of the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Flexible Agile Development Services (FADS), where tying vendor growth to unblocking competitors and increasing collective pipeline velocity changed the survival economics of the vendor pool. To operationalise this under new flexibilities such as the U.S. Revolutionary FAR Overhaul and the SHARE IT Act, it presents the Digital Public Services Readiness Model, designed to work alongside indices such as the UN Open Source Enablement Compass (OSEC) and to move procurement from buying closed artefacts to funding open capacity by evaluating the operational stewardship of private-sector service firms.

Track:
 Public Procurement Ecosystem

Two Models, One Bottleneck: Supplier Capacity as the Hidden Constraint on Open Technology Procurement

Public procurement policy increasingly opens the door to open source without solving the supplier capacity problem, and this talk presents a paper examining how two stewardship models, vendor-backed enterprise Linux and Kubernetes (SUSE, Rancher) and the community-governed Drupal project, produce or fail to produce the talent pipelines and upskilling infrastructure that public sector adoption depends on. Vendor-backed ecosystems offer a familiar risk profile of certified partners, support contracts and training paths, while community-governed ones offer a long-standing base of practitioners and institutional knowledge independent of any single commercial relationship, which public buyers rarely know how to identify, qualify or build into tenders. The paper argues that the blind spot in procurement is supplier legibility rather than policy openness: buyers lack frameworks to judge which model fits a given talent and risk context, and the upskilling pathways to make either viable once selected. Grounded in ongoing enterprise open source sales practice and long-term community involvement rather than desk research, it proposes a practical framework for evaluating supplier capacity across both models, with recommendations for building internal capability that survives staff turnover and shifting vendor relationships.

Participants are welcome to submit papers or presentations across seven key themes, which they will then present at the event:

Economic Value and Impact

As open technology’s strategic importance is increasingly recognised at the highest levels of policy, the research base must go beyond macro-level economic estimates to produce decision-maker-ready evidence that speaks to the questions procurement officers, line ministries, and budget units actually face – not just return on investment and total cost of ownership, but the political, strategic, and resilience value that conventional economic metrics fail to capture.

Governance

As major open ecosystems increasingly operate under hybrid models combining community, firm, and government oversight, the governance arrangements that determine how open technologies are stewarded, contested, and sometimes captured have outpaced the research base designed to evaluate them.

Public Procurement Ecosystem

As public procurement emerges as a critical lever for open technology adoption, the research base must address both sides of the equation: the policy and institutional conditions that open doors for open technology, and the supplier capacity, talent pipelines, and upskilling infrastructure needed to walk through them.

Sustainability and Business Models

Addressing the chronic under-resourcing of open technology ecosystems relative to the value they generate requires both stronger evidence and new institutional mechanisms that make the relationship between value extraction and contribution more legible and accountable, as well as up-to-date models of how value is created and captured in open technology markets and for open technology organisations.

Behavioural Change/Adoption

The hardest barriers to open technology deployment are organisational and cultural rather than technical, yet the research base remains thinner here than in almost any other area: what works to build internal capacity, overcome institutional resistance, and sustain momentum through leadership transitions, budget cycles, and competing priorities is still more practitioner folklore than evidence.

Security

As open technologies are deployed across an increasingly diverse range of critical contexts, the research community must continue to substantiate and refine the security case for openness across different technology types, threat models, and governance arrangements for both open source software and hardware.

Research Infrastructure

As open source tools, open data, and open standards become foundational to how knowledge is produced, shared, and validated, the research base must catch up with both their transformative role in enabling scientific discovery and collaboration, and the governance and funding arrangements needed to sustain the open infrastructure that research increasingly depends on.

What are the requirements for each submission type?

The requirements for each submission type are as follows.

  • Research Papers: No page limit, abstract of 300 words maximum
  • Presentation of Ongoing Research: 6-10 page summary, abstract of 300 words maximum
  • Topical Presentations: 2-4 page abstract or summary, 300 words maximum for initial submission

How long will my presentation be?

Presentation lengths are as follows:

  • Paper Presentation: 20 minutes + 10 mins Q&A (For completed reports and papers; Ideally suited for academic researchers, but also some non-academic researchers/practitioners)
  • Presentation of Ongoing Research: 15 minutes + 5 minutes Q&A (For ongoing research and initiatives, presented in a novel or interactive way; Ideally suited for academic and non-academic researchers, but also some practitioners)
  • Topical Presentation: 15 minutes + 5 minutes Q&A (For focused presentations on relevant ideas and concepts that are not part of ongoing research efforts; Ideally suited for non-academic researchers or practitioners, but also some academics)

Are there any submission requirements for papers, summaries, or presentations?

No! We only ask that the papers be content complete and legible, with standard font and margin sizes. Is it possible to share my paper publicly or submit it to a journal?

All reports, summaries, and presentations from the conference will optionally be made available via a dedicated Zenodo community. For more information on Zenodo, check out their website. We also recommend submitting pre-prints of your paper, even if they will later be published in journals or specific locations, to ArXiv.org, an open-access archive and free distribution service which houses nearly 2.4 million scholarly articles.
